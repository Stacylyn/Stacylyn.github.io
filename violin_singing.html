The Violin That Sang

Long before Stacylyn discovered that her voice might take her to New York City, there was another instrument that captured her heart. The violin.

Her introduction to the instrument came in fifth grade, when she was accepted into a music enrichment program funded by a grant for children from low-income families.

Her first violin wasn't exactly a Stradivarius.

It was a cigar box with strings.

For the first several weeks, that makeshift instrument was all she had to practice on. Eventually, the program provided her with a real violin of her own.

And Stacylyn practiced.

Every day.

In fact, she practiced on the school bus, both to and from school. With an hour-long ride each way, she managed to squeeze in approximately two hours of violin practice every school day.

One can only imagine how the other passengers felt about that arrangement.

But Stacylyn was falling in love with the instrument.

When her family moved to Utah, she enrolled in her junior-high orchestra. Unfortunately, her orchestra teacher didn't seem particularly impressed with his new violinist. He placed her at the back of the orchestra, where she remained largely unnoticed.

Stacylyn responded with all the maturity one might expect from a frustrated adolescent.

She became a troublemaker.

If her teacher wasn't going to recognize her talent, she was determined to demonstrate just how little she cared.

Of course, she cared enormously.

The Quartet Nobody Wanted

In eighth grade, Utah junior-high schools were invited to participate in a major music competition. Each school could select its best young musicians to represent it.

Stacylyn's teacher chose a quartet that included several of her closest friends.

He did not choose Stacylyn.

She was heartbroken.

But she wasn't the only disappointed musician. Several other students had also been passed over, and together they decided to do something rather audacious.

They would enter the competition themselves.

There was just one problem: they needed an official sponsor.

So they approached their school principal, who enthusiastically agreed to sponsor their independent quartet.

Their orchestra teacher was considerably less enthusiastic.

In fact, he laughed at their efforts and warned that they would only embarrass themselves.

Undeterred, the young musicians selected a Brandenburg piece and began rehearsing.

They had no coaching from their orchestra teacher. What they did have was determination, Stacylyn's musical ear, and an extraordinary willingness to practice.

Stacylyn remembers practicing until her fingertips bled.

Finally, competition day arrived.

And just as their teacher had predicted...

They didn't win.

But they placed higher than the quartet he had selected to represent the school.

For Stacylyn, that was victory enough.

The Soloist Nobody Heard

The following year brought a new school, a larger orchestra, and another conductor who seemed to have little interest in the freshmen seated at the back.

But this conductor already had his star.

His name was Paul Abeg.

Paul was a violin prodigy whose playing could make an instrument sound heartbreakingly beautiful. He was the orchestra's featured soloist, and Stacylyn admired him enormously.

She also had a secret ambition.

She wanted to be Paul's understudy.

There was just one difficulty.

Nobody had asked her to be his understudy.

Nobody had even auditioned her.

But Stacylyn wasn't about to let a technicality like that interfere with her plans.

She learned Paul's solos anyway, practicing until she could make her own violin sing with the same heartbreaking beauty.

When the orchestra embarked on a spring-break concert tour through California, Stacylyn brought along a rather elaborate fantasy.

Some unfortunate mishap would prevent Paul from performing. Perhaps he would break his leg. The conductor would be devastated.

What are we going to do? Who can possibly play these solos?

And from the back of the orchestra, Stacylyn would rise.

I can play them.

She would step forward, take her place before the audience, and astonish everyone.

It was a magnificent plan.

Unfortunately, Paul Abeg remained in excellent health.

He performed brilliantly at every concert.

And Stacylyn remained in the back of the orchestra, her secret talents undiscovered.

The Last Day

When the school year ended, Stacylyn faced another familiar consequence of military life.

Her family was moving again.

This time, to Washington, D.C.

She knew she would be leaving behind the orchestra, the music she loved, and the opportunity she had dreamed of all year.

On the last day of school, while the other students celebrated outside, Stacylyn quietly slipped into a practice room in the music building.

She took out her violin.

And she began playing the solos she had spent the entire year learning.

One last performance.

No audience. No conductor. No applause.

Just Stacylyn, her violin, and the heartbreaking music that seemed to express everything she was feeling.

Then, halfway through her playing, the door opened.

It was her orchestra conductor.

He stood listening in astonishment.

"I thought you were Paul," he said.

Then he said it again.

He could scarcely believe what he was hearing.

Finally, he asked her how long she had been able to play like that.

"All year long," Stacylyn answered.

The conductor immediately offered her second chair beside Paul for the following school year.

At last, she had been heard.

But there was a problem.

She wouldn't be returning.

Her family was moving to Washington, D.C.

The conductor wasn't prepared to let her go so easily.

He asked permission to speak with her parents and made an extraordinary offer: Stacylyn could remain in Utah and live with him and his wife. He would personally continue her training, believing that within another year she could be ready to play with the Utah Symphony.

Her parents left the decision to her.

For a young violinist who had spent years longing to be recognized, it was almost everything she had ever wanted.

But accepting meant leaving her family behind.

And Stacylyn wasn't certain that was the right choice.

So she chose her family.

The Music That Remained

When Stacylyn arrived in Washington, D.C., she discovered that her new high school had no orchestra. There were no opportunities for her to continue playing, and her parents couldn't afford private lessons.

Her violin remained packed away.

She thought about it often. About the orchestra, the solos, the music she had loved so deeply, and the future she might have had.

But she never took it out of its case.

She never played the violin again.

Yet the music never left her.

She carried with her the sound of the orchestra, the beauty of classical music, and the extraordinary feeling of making an instrument express emotions that words could never quite capture.

And when she sang, she carried that same musical instinct with her.

She sang the way she had played her violin — shaping phrases, following the movement of the music, and searching for that elusive beauty that could make a melody seem almost human.

The violin had taught her something she would carry into every performance that followed.

An instrument could sing. And so could she.
