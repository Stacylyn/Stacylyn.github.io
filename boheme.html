The Story of La Bohème

Set in the Latin Quarter of 19th-century Paris, Puccini’s beloved La Bohème follows four struggling young artists who share a cold attic apartment. Despite their poverty, they embrace life with laughter and friendship. Amid the intoxicating joy of young love, they find warmth in one another and dream of a brighter future.

On Christmas Eve, the poet Rodolfo meets Mimì, a gentle young seamstress who comes to his door seeking a light for her extinguished candle. Instantly enchanted by her, Rodolfo sets out to win her heart and soon discovers that she is equally drawn to his warmth and sincerity. Meanwhile, his best-friend Marcello rekindles his tempestuous romance with the spirited Musetta.

But love alone cannot protect Rodolfo and Mimì from the harsh realities of poverty and illness. Mimì’s health declines, and Rodolfo is tormented by his inability to provide the warmth and care she desperately needs. Knowing how deeply he suffers, Mimì lovingly chooses to release him from their relationship, assuring him that she leaves with no bitterness in her heart. Yet in sacrificing her own happiness for his sake, she may unknowingly deprive him of the very thing he would sacrifice everything to preserve—her life.

Behind the Scenes

My Experience Performing Mimì

My journey with Puccini's La Bohème

Preparing for the Role

I had just completed a wonderfully successful run as Cio-Cio-San in Puccini's Madama Butterfly when the director of the opera company approached me with a proposition. Would I be willing to stay with the company and serve as the cover for the title role in Suor Angelica? And if I agreed, the company would offer me the leading role of Mimì in its upcoming production of La Bohème.

I was absolutely floored!

You have to understand something about Mimì. She is one of the most coveted roles in the soprano repertoire. Unlike many operatic heroines whose roles demand a particular vocal classification, Mimì is remarkably versatile. Lyric sopranos, light lyric sopranos, lyric-spinto sopranos, dramatic sopranos, and even certain high mezzos can all bring something beautiful and distinctive to the character.

Which means the competition is enormous! You're not simply competing against other sopranos with voices similar to yours. You're potentially competing against practically every female opera singer in New York City! And the company was already holding auditions. I could only imagine how many talented singers were hoping for the opportunity to sing Mimì.

Yet here I was, being offered the role without even auditioning. And I would get to cover Suor Angelica as well? Are you kidding me? I felt like the luckiest soprano in New York City.

But along with the excitement came something else... Pressure.

You see, Mimì isn't a princess. She isn't a queen, a celebrated beauty, or a woman of wealth and influence. She's a poor young seamstress living in a cold little room in Paris, struggling to survive in a city that barely notices her.

She's a nobody. But she has a gorgeous voice and something to say.

Her beauty isn't announced by a royal entrance or an elaborate costume. It reveals itself the moment she begins to sing. Her voice must be so captivating, so sincere, and so effortlessly beautiful that both Rodolfo and the audience fall in love with her almost immediately. There is nowhere to hide… Mimì's strength lies in her vulnerability, her tenderness, and her quiet dignity. And that meant I couldn't simply sing the role well. I had to make the audience fall in love with an ordinary girl.

I knew I had been given an extraordinary opportunity. Now I endeavored to deserve it.

Rehearsals and Collaboration: Bringing "La Bohème" to life... a little bit of heaven and a whole lot of chaos.

Yes, there was a certain moment that remains forever in my memory. It happened during the music rehearsal of the famous Café Momus scene. It's the scene where Puccini has as many as seven characters singing their individual thoughts, conversations, and emotions—all at the same time.

Now, imagine seven actors in a play speaking their private thoughts simultaneously. It would be absolute chaos! Nobody would understand a word.

But in opera? It's glorious!

There I was, standing in the middle of the most beautiful heaven of music and glorious voices, each singing something entirely different, yet somehow coming together in this magnificent tapestry of sound.

And I started to cry. Do you have any idea how difficult it is to sing high notes when you're weeping? I was so overcome with emotion. I couldn't help myself. I was standing right in the middle of Puccini's music, surrounded by all that beauty, and it was simply overwhelming.

There were other moments like that throughout our rehearsals. Moments of sheer joy when the voices came together and I found myself completely enveloped in the glory of the music.

And that was before we added the orchestra!

Of course, not everything about preparing La Bohème was quite so heavenly.

The Art of Organized Chaos

If the music rehearsals were heaven, the staging rehearsals occasionally felt like something considerably less divine! The Café Momus scene is a perfect example.

Imagine trying to choreograph an entire bustling Parisian street scene, complete with principal characters, waiters, townspeople, vendors, and a chorus of singers, all moving about as though they were simply enjoying an ordinary evening in Paris.

Except nothing about it was ordinary. Every entrance, every movement, every interaction had to be carefully planned. The director would spend what seemed like an eternity giving individual instructions to performers while the rest of us stood patiently in our assigned places.

And stood. And stood.

Occasionally, when we finally attempted to put all those carefully choreographed movements together, there would be an unexpected collision as two performers discovered they were occupying or passing through precisely the same spot! So much for the magic of opera.

But gradually, the pieces began to fit. The traffic flowed. The conversations started to look organic. The café came alive. What had once been a tedious collection of individual instructions became a vibrant, bustling world filled with people who seemed to have lives of their own.

Absolutely thrilling!

Every Performer Is a Leading Performer

There was something else about this production that mattered enormously to me, and it had very little to do with the music. It had to do with the people.

Over the years, I'd noticed that the atmosphere of an opera production was often established by the people in the leading roles. Yet, oftentimes that atmosphere resembled a strict hierarchy. The principal singers occupied the top of the admiration-and-respect ladder, while the chorus and crew were treated like the bottom rung.

I had never particularly liked that arrangement, because I believe it takes the whole company to make a production shine. There's an old theatrical saying, often attributed to Stanislavski: "There are no small parts, only small actors." I had always believed that. And now, for the second time in this company, I was the leading lady.

I had an opportunity to do things differently. So I did.

I would sometimes join the chorus and sing with them, simply because it was fun! During breaks in our combined rehearsals, I made a point of spending time with the chorus members, getting to know them and enjoying their company. More importantly, I wanted everyone to understand something I believed with all my heart:

Every person in this production was the leading performer of their own role.

It didn't matter whether you were singing Mimì, Rodolfo, Musetta, or a single line in the chorus. You had an important contribution to make.

Because here's the thing about opera: a leading singer can give the performance of a lifetime, but if the world surrounding that character isn't equally convincing, the entire production suffers. On the other hand, when the chorus is magnificent, when the supporting performers are fully invested, when every person on that stage brings their very best, something extraordinary happens. The whole opera becomes spectacular. That was the atmosphere I wanted us to create.

I made a promise to my fellow performers: I would give everything I had to my role if they would give everything they had to theirs. No one in our company was more important than anyone else. We would either fly together or sink together.

And something wonderful happened.

Something Greater Than the Sum of Our Parts

Our cast became one of the most close-knit ensembles I had ever worked with.

There was a genuine sense of camaraderie, mutual respect, and shared purpose. We weren't simply a collection of individual singers trying to make a good impression. We were a company of artists working together to create something extraordinary and beautiful. Everyone brought their A-game.

You could feel it in rehearsals. You could see it in the performances. And ultimately, you could read it in the reviews.

Our production was enthusiastically received, and the praise from both audience and press reflected the extraordinary collective effort that had gone into bringing Puccini's masterpiece to life.

I am enormously proud of what we accomplished.

But when I think back on La Bohème, the memory that returns most vividly isn't the applause or the reviews.

It's that moment in rehearsal when I stood surrounded by voices, listening to Puccini's music soar around me, and found myself crying because it was simply too beautiful not to.

And perhaps that's what made our production so special. We weren't just singing about love, friendship, sacrifice, and the fleeting beauty of life. In our own little way, we were living some of those very things together.

And for a little while, we made something truly glorious.
